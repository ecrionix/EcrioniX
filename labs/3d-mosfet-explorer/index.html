What is a MOSFET?

A MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) is a four-terminal, voltage-controlled semiconductor switching and amplifying device. Invented conceptually by Julius Edgar Lilienfeld in the 1920s and first physically fabricated by Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs in 1959, the MOSFET has become the fundamental building block of modern digital electronics, microprocessors, memory arrays, and analog integrated circuits.

Unlike a Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)—which requires continuous base current to sustain collector conduction—the MOSFET relies on the field effect. A voltage applied to the gate terminal creates an electrostatic electric field that penetrates an insulating dielectric layer, modulating the conductivity of a silicon channel without drawing sustained direct current (DC).

MOSFET Physical Construction & Anatomy

A standard planar enhancement-mode MOSFET consists of six primary physical regions:

  • Substrate / Body (B): The bulk semiconductor wafer that provides structural support and the background crystalline lattice. For an NMOS, the substrate is lightly doped p-type silicon; for a PMOS, an n-type substrate or n-well is used.
  • Source (S): A heavily doped semiconductor region ($n^+$ in NMOS, $p^+$ in PMOS) that acts as the reservoir and emitter of charge carriers into the channel.
  • Drain (D): A heavily doped semiconductor region ($n^+$ in NMOS, $p^+$ in PMOS) positioned opposite the source that collects carriers emerging from the channel.
  • Gate Dielectric / Oxide: An ultra-thin insulating layer (historically thermally grown silicon dioxide, $ ext{SiO}_2$; in advanced technologies, high-$\kappa$ dielectrics such as hafnium oxide, $ ext{HfO}_2$) positioned directly over the channel region.
  • Gate Electrode (G): A conductive plate (heavily doped polysilicon or work-function engineered metal) deposited directly onto the gate dielectric.
  • Inversion Channel: The conductive surface sheet that dynamically forms directly beneath the gate oxide when sufficient gate bias attracts mobile carriers to bridge the gap between source and drain.

How an NMOS Transistor Works

In an n-channel enhancement MOSFET (NMOS), the substrate consists of p-type silicon containing abundant positive holes and very few free conduction electrons. Two separate $n^+$ regions (the source and drain) are diffused into the substrate, forming two back-to-back $pn$ junctions that naturally block current flow at zero gate bias.

1. Cutoff State ($V_{GS} < V_{TH}$)

When the gate-to-source voltage $V_{GS}$ is below the threshold voltage $V_{TH}$, the vertical electric field is insufficient to invert the silicon surface. The two back-to-back $pn$ junctions between the $n^+$ wells and the p-substrate remain in place, presenting a high-resistance barrier. Only subthreshold diffusion leakage current ($I_{sub} pprox 0$) flows.

2. Threshold & Channel Inversion ($V_{GS} \ge V_{TH}$)

Applying a positive voltage to the gate repels mobile holes away from the silicon surface into the substrate bulk, leaving behind negatively charged, immobile ionized acceptor atoms (the depletion region). As $V_{GS}$ continues to rise past $V_{TH}$, the surface electrostatic potential reaches twice the bulk Fermi potential ($\psi_s pprox 2\phi_B$). Mobile minority electrons are drawn to the surface, creating an n-type inversion channel that electrically shorts the $n^+$ source to the $n^+$ drain.

3. Conduction Under Drain Bias ($V_{DS} > 0$)

Once the inversion channel is established, applying a positive voltage to the drain relative to the source creates a lateral electric field. Negatively charged electrons drift from the source, across the channel, and into the drain. By historical convention, conventional drain current ($I_D$) flows in the opposite direction—entering the drain and exiting the source.

How a PMOS Transistor Works

A p-channel enhancement MOSFET (PMOS) operates on complementary physics. Built in an n-type substrate (or an n-well within a p-substrate), its source and drain are heavily doped $p^+$ regions:

  • Gate Voltage Polarity: To turn a PMOS ON, the gate must be biased negative relative to the source ($V_{GS} < V_{TH,p}$, where $V_{TH,p}$ is negative, e.g. $-0.7 ext{ V}$).
  • Charge Carriers: The negative gate attracts positive holes to the surface, forming a p-type inversion channel bridging the $p^+$ source and $p^+$ drain.
  • Current Direction: Conventional current flows from the source into the drain ($I_{SD}$).
  • Carrier Mobility Difference: In silicon, hole mobility ($\mu_p pprox 450 ext{ cm}^2/ ext{V}\cdot ext{s}$) is significantly lower than electron mobility ($\mu_n pprox 1350 ext{ cm}^2/ ext{V}\cdot ext{s}$). Consequently, to achieve equal current drive and switching speed, a PMOS transistor must be fabricated roughly 2 to 2.5 times wider ($W_p pprox 2.5 imes W_n$) than an NMOS transistor.

What is Threshold Voltage & Channel Inversion?

The threshold voltage ($V_{TH}$) is the critical gate-to-source bias required to transition the silicon surface from depletion into strong inversion. Mathematically, it is determined by the flat-band voltage, the work function difference between the gate electrode and silicon substrate, the fixed oxide charge, and the depletion layer charge:

VTH = VFB + 2φB + √(2q · εSi · NA · 2φB) / Cox

When the substrate body is biased relative to the source ($V_{SB} > 0$), the depletion region expands, causing the threshold voltage to increase via the Body Effect:

ΔVTH = γ · [ √(2φB + VSB) - √(2φB) ]

where $\gamma$ is the body-effect parameter and $\phi_B$ is the bulk Fermi potential.

Gate Oxide & High-κ Dielectric Engineering

The gate oxide represents one of the most critical engineering feats in semiconductor fabrication. Historically composed of silicon dioxide ($ ext{SiO}_2$), thermal oxidation produced an interface with silicon with virtually zero defect trap states.

However, as transistor gate lengths scaled below 90 nanometers, the physical thickness of the $ ext{SiO}_2$ dielectric dropped below 1.2 nanometers—equivalent to just 4 or 5 atomic layers of silicon dioxide. At this microscopic thickness, electrons quantum-mechanically tunneled directly through the insulator, creating unsustainable static gate leakage current.

To solve this, the semiconductor industry introduced High-κ Metal Gate (HKMG) technology. By replacing $ ext{SiO}_2$ ($\kappa pprox 3.9$) with hafnium dioxide ($ ext{HfO}_2$, $\kappa pprox 25$), the physical dielectric layer could be manufactured substantially thicker to block quantum tunneling while preserving the same high capacitive coupling ($C_{ox} = rac{\kappa arepsilon_0}{t_{ox}}$) required to invert the channel.

MOSFET vs Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)

Understanding the architectural differences between field-effect and bipolar devices explains why MOSFETs dominate digital computing while BJTs excel in specific RF and linear applications:

Parameter MOSFET (Field-Effect) BJT (Bipolar Junction)
Control Mechanism Voltage-controlled ($V_{GS}$) via electric field. Current-controlled ($I_B$) via base carrier injection.
DC Input Impedance Extremely high ($> 10^{12}\ \Omega$), negligible DC gate current. Low to moderate ($1\ ext{k}\Omega - 100\ ext{k}\Omega$), requires base current.
Carrier Type Unipolar (electrons in NMOS, holes in PMOS). Bipolar (both majority and minority carriers).
Thermal Stability Negative temperature coefficient at high current; inherently immune to thermal runaway. Positive temperature coefficient; susceptible to thermal runaway if uncompensated.
IC Density & Fabrication Simple self-aligned planar process; billions fit on a single chip. Complex isolation wells; lower density, higher silicon area per device.
Static Power Consumption Virtually zero in complementary CMOS configuration. Continuous static bias current required in active state.

Applications in CMOS Integrated Circuits

The crowning achievement of modern semiconductor design is CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor) architecture, which pairs an NMOS pull-down network with a PMOS pull-up network:

  • CMOS Inverter: When the input is HIGH ($V_{DD}$), the NMOS conducts (pulling the output to Ground/0V) while the PMOS is OFF. When the input is LOW (0V), the PMOS conducts (pulling the output to $V_{DD}$) while the NMOS is OFF.
  • Zero Static Dissipation: In either steady-state output condition, there is never a direct DC conduction path between $V_{DD}$ and Ground. Power is drawn strictly during high-to-low and low-to-high switching transitions to charge load capacitances ($P_{dyn} = C_L V_{DD}^2 f$).
  • Logic Gates: NAND, NOR, XOR, flip-flops, SRAM memory cells (6T SRAM), and processor ALUs are constructed from interconnected CMOS pairs.

From Planar MOSFET to 3D FinFET and GAA Nanosheets

For over forty years, Moore's Law progressed by shrinking planar MOSFET dimensions. However, at gate lengths below 20 nanometers, classical planar MOSFETs suffered severe short-channel effects (SCE): the drain's electric field began competing with the gate, causing Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL) and punch-through leakage even when the transistor was ostensibly OFF.

To restore electrostatic gate control, the industry revolutionized transistor architecture:

  • FinFET (3D Tri-Gate): Introduced in 2011 at the 22nm node, the silicon channel was raised vertically into a thin "fin" wrapped on three sides by the gate oxide and electrode, drastically suppressing sub-surface leakage.
  • GAAFET (Gate-All-Around Nanosheet): At the 3nm and 2nm nodes, the channel is split into multiple stacked horizontal silicon nanosheets completely encircled on all four sides by the gate, maximizing electrostatic gate control and driving drive current per unit footprint.
  • CFET (Complementary FET): The upcoming 3D transistor scaling frontier, stacking PMOS nanosheets directly on top of NMOS nanosheets to halve the standard logic cell footprint.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. How does a MOSFET transistor turn ON?

In an enhancement-mode NMOS transistor, applying a positive gate-to-source voltage (VGS) exceeding the threshold voltage (VTH) establishes a vertical electric field across the insulating gate oxide. This field repels majority holes from the p-type silicon surface and attracts mobile minority electrons, forming a continuous n-type conductive inversion channel connecting the source and drain.

Q2. What is the difference between NMOS and PMOS transistors?

An NMOS transistor uses a p-type substrate with heavily doped n+ source and drain regions, where mobile electrons form the inversion channel when positive gate voltage is applied (VGS > VTH). A PMOS transistor uses an n-type substrate or n-well with heavily doped p+ source and drain regions, requiring a negative gate-to-source voltage to attract positive holes into the channel. In silicon, electron mobility is roughly 2.5 times higher than hole mobility, making NMOS transistors inherently faster and lower in on-resistance than PMOS devices of equal physical dimensions.

Q3. What is the pinch-off effect in the MOSFET saturation region?

As the drain-to-source voltage (VDS) increases, the local voltage across the gate oxide near the drain end decreases: VGD = VGS - VDS. When VDS reaches VGS - VTH, the oxide voltage at the drain drops below the threshold voltage required to sustain inversion, causing the channel thickness to narrow to zero (pinch-off). Beyond pinch-off (saturation region), electrons are swept across the remaining high-field depletion region, causing drain current (ID) to level off and become virtually independent of further increases in VDS.

Q4. What is the function of the gate oxide in a MOSFET?

The gate oxide is an ultra-thin insulating dielectric layer (such as silicon dioxide SiO2 or high-k hafnium dioxide HfO2) separating the gate electrode from the silicon channel. It provides pure electrostatic capacitive control over the channel while preventing DC current conduction into the gate, resulting in near-infinite DC input resistance.

Q5. What is threshold voltage (VTH)?

Threshold voltage (VTH) is the minimum gate-to-source voltage required to induce strong inversion at the semiconductor surface beneath the gate oxide, transitioning the transistor from an insulating cutoff state to a conductive channel state.

Q6. What are the three fundamental MOSFET operating regions?

The three regions are: 1. Cutoff (VGS <= VTH), where no conductive channel exists and ID is approximately zero; 2. Triode or Linear region (VGS > VTH and VDS < VGS - VTH), where the channel acts like a voltage-controlled resistor; 3. Saturation region (VGS > VTH and VDS >= VGS - VTH), where channel pinch-off occurs at the drain and drain current saturates.

Q7. How does electron flow differ from conventional current in a MOSFET?

In an NMOS transistor, negatively charged electrons physically drift from the source (negative/ground potential) toward the drain (positive potential). By historical scientific convention, conventional electrical current (ID) is defined as the direction of positive charge movement, meaning conventional current enters the drain and exits the source.

Q8. Why do modern microprocessors use CMOS instead of only NMOS?

Complementary MOS (CMOS) pairs NMOS pull-down transistors with PMOS pull-up transistors. In any steady logic state (0 or 1), one transistor is completely OFF while the other is ON, resulting in virtually zero static DC power dissipation. Power is consumed primarily during dynamic switching transients when charging and discharging parasitic nodal capacitances.
