Lesson 4.10

Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS) Basics

Everything this module has covered — skew, jitter, uncertainty, gating, generated clocks — describes how STA measures and models clock behavior. Clock Tree Synthesis is the physical design step that actually builds the buffer network responsible for creating that behavior in silicon, and its goals are defined almost entirely in terms of the STA metrics from the earlier lessons in this module.

1. The problem CTS solves

Before CTS runs, a design's clock is a single logical net connecting a source pin to potentially hundreds of thousands of flip-flop clock pins scattered across the die. Driving that fan-out directly from one buffer is physically impossible — the capacitive load would be enormous and the resulting rise/fall time would be far too slow for any reasonable clock frequency. CTS builds a distribution network of buffers (and sometimes inverters) that fans the clock signal out in stages, each stage driving a manageable load, until every flop's clock pin is reached with an acceptably fast edge and acceptably low, balanced latency.

3. Topology choices: H-tree, X-tree, and mesh
  • H-tree (and its generalizations, X-tree/fishbone): a recursively symmetric branching structure where every leaf is, by construction, an equal number of branch levels and (ideally) equal wire length from the root. This naturally minimizes skew by geometric symmetry rather than by tuning individual buffer sizes. Classic choice for regular, grid-like designs (memory arrays, regular datapath blocks).
  • Clock mesh: instead of a strict tree, the final distribution layer is a shorted grid of wires driven by many buffers in parallel. Because the grid is shorted, local skew is averaged out by the low resistance of the mesh itself — extremely robust to local variation and even to a single buffer failure, at the cost of significantly higher clock power (many buffers driving a low-impedance shorted network) and more complex extraction/analysis. Used on the most skew-sensitive, highest-performance domains (CPU cores, high-speed serial links).
  • General buffered tree (non-symmetric, algorithmically balanced): the most common real-world case for irregular, standard-cell-dominated SoC logic. CTS tools use algorithms (commonly variants of the classic "Deferred-Merge Embedding" / DME family) to place buffers and route wires so that latencies balance out even though the physical layout is not geometrically symmetric like an H-tree.
4. The CTS + STA feedback loop

CTS does not run once and finish — it runs, then hands its result to STA for a full skew/latency/transition report, and iterates based on what STA finds:

  1. Initial CTS pass builds a first buffered tree targeting a global skew and latency budget derived from pre-CTS clock uncertainty assumptions (Lesson 4.5).
  2. Post-CTS STA reports the real, achieved skew per register pair, real transition times at every clock pin, and — critically — any hold violations exposed now that real (non-ideal) clock latency exists.
  3. CTS optimization (ECO) targets specific problem branches identified by STA: adding useful skew on setup-critical branches, adding delay buffers on hold-critical branches, resizing buffers where transition targets are missed.
  4. Re-run STA to confirm the fix didn't create a new violation elsewhere (a very common outcome — fixing hold on one branch by adding delay can push a different, previously-fine path on the same branch into a setup violation).

This loop is essentially the same setup/hold-fixing methodology covered in Lessons 12 and 13, but applied specifically to the clock network's own buffers rather than to combinational data-path logic.

5. CTS-aware SDC: exclude clock nets from optimization elsewhere
# Ensure synthesis/PnR treat the clock network specially — # don't let general logic optimization touch clock buffers, # and set explicit transition/capacitance targets for the tree: set_clock_transition 0.08 [get_clocks CLK_CORE] set_max_transition 0.10 [get_clocks CLK_CORE] -clock_path set_dont_touch [get_nets -of_objects [get_pins u_pll/CLK_OUT]]

These constraints keep general-purpose logic synthesis and optimization from accidentally resizing or removing clock buffers as if they were ordinary datapath cells — the clock network is architecturally special and needs its own dedicated CTS engine and its own dedicated SDC guardrails throughout the flow.
