Lesson 4.2

Clock Skew (Positive vs. Negative)

Clock skew is the difference in clock arrival time between the launch flop and the capture flop of a timing path. It sounds like a second-order effect, but skew single-handedly determines whether a design closes setup at a high frequency or bleeds hold violations across every corner — sometimes both at once.

1. Definition: skew is a difference, not a delay

Every flip-flop in a synchronous design is driven by a clock signal that has traveled some distance from the clock source (PLL, oscillator, or clock pin) through buffers, inverters, and wire segments. That travel time is clock latency. Two flops almost never see the clock edge at exactly the same instant, because their physical location on the die differs and the clock tree branches that feed them differ in buffer count, wire length, and loading.

Clock skew is defined between a specific launch flop (start of a timing path) and a specific capture flop (end of the path):

Skew = Clock_Latency(capture) - Clock_Latency(launch)

Skew is always evaluated per path, not per design. A chip does not have "one skew value" — it has a different skew for every register pair that forms a timing arc, because different pairs sit on different clock tree branches. When engineers say "the design has 80ps of skew," they usually mean the global skew — the maximum skew observed across all register-to-register paths, which is what limits achievable frequency in the worst case.

Common misconception: Skew is not the same as clock jitter. Skew is a static, spatial difference caused by clock tree topology (same clock edge arriving at different times at different flops). Jitter is a temporal, cycle-to-cycle variation of a single clock edge's own position (see Lesson 4.3). Both eat into timing margin, but they come from entirely different physical mechanisms and are modeled differently in STA.
2. Sign convention: positive vs. negative skew

Sign convention in skew is defined relative to the direction of data flow — from launch to capture:

Skew typeMeaningEffect on setupEffect on hold
Positive skewCapture clock arrives later than launch clockHelps — effectively extends the clock period available for the data pathHurts — widens the window in which new data can race in before the old value is safely latched
Negative skewCapture clock arrives earlier than launch clockHurts — shrinks the effective period, tightening setup marginHelps — the capture edge has already passed by the time new data could possibly arrive, giving the hold check more margin
Zero skew (ideal)Both edges arrive simultaneouslyNo skew contribution either wayNo skew contribution either way

This is the single most misunderstood point for engineers new to STA: skew that helps setup always hurts hold on the exact same path, and vice versa. They cannot both be fixed favorably by moving skew in one direction — you are always trading one check for the other on a given register pair. This is why STA engineers talk about "skew budgeting" rather than "eliminating skew": the goal is to place skew where it does the most good and the least harm across the whole design, not to drive it to zero everywhere.

3. Why positive skew helps setup — the equation

Recall the setup slack equation from Lesson 2 of this course:

Setup Slack = (T_clk + Skew - T_setup) - T_data_path

Here Skew = Latency(capture) - Latency(launch). If skew is positive (capture clock is later), it adds directly to the effective clock period the data path is allowed to use. A design with a genuinely tight critical path can sometimes be pushed to close setup purely by deliberately routing more clock latency to the capture flop — this is a legitimate, commonly used physical design technique called useful skew (covered in Lesson 4.4), and it is applied automatically by modern CTS (clock tree synthesis) engines during optimization.

But the same equation shows the danger: if that same register pair also has a hold check (and register-to-register paths always do — see Lesson 2), the positive skew that helped setup is now working against the hold margin on the very next cycle boundary.

4. Why positive skew hurts hold — the equation
Hold Slack = T_data_path - (T_hold + Skew)

Hold checks have no clock period term — they are a same-edge, race-condition check between the launch edge and the capture edge of the same clock cycle. If the capture clock arrives later (positive skew), the effective time available for new data to sneak into the capture flop's D pin before the (delayed) capture edge shrinks. In the worst case, if skew exceeds the data path delay minus hold time, the flop captures the new value instead of the value that was supposed to be launched the previous cycle — a functional failure, not just a timing miss, because the flop effectively skips a data value entirely.

Why hold violations are more dangerous than setup violations in silicon: A setup violation can sometimes be worked around by simply reducing clock frequency — the chip still functions correctly, just slower. A hold violation is frequency-independent: it will fail at any clock speed, including DC, because it is a race condition intrinsic to the data and clock paths themselves. This is why hold closure is mandatory before tapeout, while setup timing can occasionally ship with known margin if the part is speed-binned.
5. Physical sources of skew
  • Clock tree topology imbalance — unbalanced buffer counts or wire lengths between the clock source and different flops. The dominant contributor in most designs; CTS tools work specifically to minimize this.
  • On-die process variation (OCV) — even an intentionally balanced clock tree has real skew once you account for transistor and wire variation across the die (see Lesson 3.2 on OCV/AOCV/POCV). Two "identical" buffers on different parts of the die do not switch at exactly the same speed.
  • Voltage (IR) drop — regions of the chip under heavier switching activity see local supply droop, slowing clock buffers in that region relative to others.
  • Temperature gradients — hot spots near high-power logic blocks change local cell delay, adding skew that a uniform-temperature model would miss (addressed by gradient-aware or "thermal-aware" STA in advanced flows).
  • Deliberate CTS insertion (useful skew) — the physical design tool intentionally unbalances the tree on specific branches to fix a setup-critical path, accepting the hold cost on that branch because margin exists there.
6. Skew in a PrimeTime timing report

When you run report_timing, the clock network delay lines for the launch and capture branches are reported separately, and the difference between them is the skew for that specific path (plus any explicit uncertainty applied on top):

Startpoint: reg_A (rising edge-triggered flip-flop clocked by CLK) Endpoint: reg_B (rising edge-triggered flip-flop clocked by CLK) Path Group: CLK Path Type: max (Setup Check) Point Incr Path ----------------------------------------------------------- clock CLK (rise edge) 0.00 0.00 clock network delay (propagated) 0.62 0.62 <- launch clock latency reg_A/CP 0.00 0.62 r reg_A/Q 0.11 0.73 f U1/Y 0.24 0.97 r reg_B/D 0.00 0.97 f data arrival time 0.97 clock CLK (rise edge) 1.00 1.00 clock network delay (propagated) 0.79 1.79 <- capture clock latency clock reconvergence pessimism 0.00 1.79 clock uncertainty -0.05 1.74 reg_B/CP 1.74 r library setup time -0.09 1.65 data required time 1.65 ----------------------------------------------------------- data required time 1.65 data arrival time -0.97 ----------------------------------------------------------- slack (MET) 0.68

Here, launch latency is 0.62ns and capture latency is 0.79ns, giving +0.17ns of positive skew in favor of the setup check on this path — you can see it baked directly into the larger "clock CLK (rise edge)" + "clock network delay" arithmetic on the capture side. If you ran a hold check (-delay_type min) on this exact same register pair, that same +0.17ns would subtract directly from hold slack instead.

7. Skew budgeting in practice

Real SoCs do not try to drive skew to zero everywhere — that is neither achievable nor optimal. Instead, physical design and STA teams work from a skew budget:

  • Identify paths with large positive setup slack margin (the data path is much faster than required) — these can tolerate negative skew without hurting setup, and negative skew there actively helps their hold margin.
  • Identify paths that are setup-critical (near-zero or negative setup slack) — CTS is directed to add useful (positive) skew specifically on these branches, borrowing time from the clock tree instead of the logic.
  • Verify that the useful skew added for setup doesn't push any hold check on the same branch into violation — this is the core iterative loop of physical timing closure covered in Lessons 12 and 13.
Rule of thumb used by CTS engines: useful skew is typically capped at a small fraction of the clock period (often 5-15%) specifically because larger skew values create hold risk that is far more expensive to fix later (buffer insertion, area, power) than the setup problem it solved.
