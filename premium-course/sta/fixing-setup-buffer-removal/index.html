Lesson 9.2

Fixing Setup: Buffer Removal & Cloning

Sometimes tools insert too many buffers to fix hold or transition issues, leading to setup violations. We fix these by ripping out buffers, or by cloning logic to split fan-out burdens.

1

Buffer Removal

Buffers exist primarily to improve slew, drive large nets, or fix hold violations. However, every buffer has an intrinsic delay. If a data path has a tight setup margin, unnecessary buffers kill timing.

The Irony of Buffering

Removing a buffer eliminates its intrinsic cell delay, but worsens the transition time of the previous cell because it now sees a larger capacitance. You remove a buffer only when the intrinsic delay saved is greater than the slew degradation penalty.

STA tools will highlight long chains of buffers (buffer trees) in the critical path. During ECO, replacing two cascaded buffers with a single, stronger buffer or removing them entirely can rescue picoseconds of setup margin.

2

Logic Cloning (Splitting Fan-out)

When a cell drives 20 flip-flops, the huge wire capacitance and pin capacitance slow it down significantly, causing setup violations at the destinations.

Cloning: Instead of relying on one huge driver, we duplicate (clone) the logic gate. The original gate drives 10 flops, and the clone drives the other 10. The load is halved, improving the delay for both paths.

Cloning Constraints: You cannot clone logic that has state (like flip-flops or latches) lightly, because ensuring the states remain synchronized takes extreme care. Cloning is almost always restricted to combinatorial logic.
