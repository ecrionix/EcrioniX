Module 1, Lesson 2: Clocks, Skew, and Jitter

1. Ideal Clocks vs. Real Clocks

In the pre-layout phase of digital design, Static Timing Analysis (STA) initially assumes an ideal clock network. An ideal clock has zero insertion delay, zero skew, and zero transition time, meaning the clock edge arrives at every sequential element in the design at the exact same instant.

However, physical reality is much more complex. After Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS), we must analyze the design using real clocks. A real clock signal originates from a source (like a PLL) and travels through a complex, balanced tree of buffers and metal routing to reach thousands or millions of sequential elements.

Real Clock Anomalies:
  • Insertion Delay (Latency): The time it takes for the clock signal to travel from the clock source to the clock pin of a specific register.
  • Skew: The difference in clock arrival times between two communicating registers.
  • Jitter: The dynamic, cycle-to-cycle variation in the clock period caused by unpredictable noise and interference.
2. Clock Skew: The Double-Edged Sword

Clock Skew is the relative difference in insertion delay between the capture register's clock and the launch register's clock:

Skew = Capture_Clock_Delay - Launch_Clock_Delay

Depending on the routing of the clock tree and the physical location of the registers, skew can be mathematically positive or negative. Let's visualize how the capture clock edge shifts relative to the launch clock edge:

Positive Skew

Occurs when the clock arrives at the capture flip-flop later than at the launch flip-flop. The capture clock edge is delayed relative to the launch clock edge.

  • Impact on Setup: Helps Setup Time. The later capture edge gives the data signal more time to travel through the combinational logic. It artificially extends the clock period available for that specific path.
  • Impact on Hold: Hurts Hold Time. The later capture edge means the data from the same clock cycle might race through the logic and incorrectly overwrite the capture flop before it has a chance to sample the previous cycle's data.

Negative Skew

Occurs when the clock arrives at the capture flip-flop earlier than at the launch flip-flop.

  • Impact on Setup: Hurts Setup Time. The earlier capture edge truncates the time available for data to propagate, making setup requirements stricter. If data doesn't arrive fast enough, a setup violation occurs.
  • Impact on Hold: Helps Hold Time. Since the capture flop samples earlier, it is less likely that the new data launched on the same edge will race through and corrupt the hold time check.
// Mathematical impact of skew on Slack: Setup_Slack = Clock_Period - Setup_Time - Max_Data_Delay + Skew Hold_Slack = Min_Data_Delay - Hold_Time - Skew
3. Clock Jitter and Uncertainty

Unlike skew, which is a static spatial variation (a delay difference that remains relatively constant between two locations), Jitter is a dynamic temporal variation (a timing difference that changes over time). It is the deviation of the clock edge from its ideal periodic position.

Jitter is primarily caused by physical and electrical variations in the chip:

  • Power supply noise and dynamic IR drop
  • Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) intrinsic noise
  • Cross-talk from adjacent aggressively switching signals
  • Thermal variations across the die
Important Note: In Static Timing Analysis, we cannot predict exact jitter values for every clock cycle. Instead, we model jitter—along with other margin requirements—using clock uncertainty.

In STA tools, uncertainty is subtracted from the required time for Setup checks (making the check more pessimistic). For hold checks, uncertainty is added to the required time.

# Applying uncertainty in SDC (Synopsys Design Constraints) # Example: 100ps uncertainty for setup, 50ps for hold set_clock_uncertainty -setup 0.100 [get_clocks SYS_CLK] set_clock_uncertainty -hold 0.050 [get_clocks SYS_CLK]

By defining uncertainty, you force the STA tool to build a safety margin into all timing paths, ensuring the chip will function reliably even when clock edges fluctuate in real silicon.
