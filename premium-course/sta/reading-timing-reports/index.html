Introduction to Timing Reports

In industry-standard Static Timing Analysis (STA) tools like Synopsys PrimeTime or Cadence Tempus, the timing report is the ultimate source of truth for the timing health of your design. Understanding how to decipher these reports efficiently is a critical skill for any ASIC/FPGA design or verification engineer. A typical timing report details the timing path with the worst slack (or a specific path requested by the user), breaking down the delay through every gate and net along the way.

The report not only tells you if your design met timing, but why it failed, pointing you directly to the structural or physical bottleneck.

Anatomy of a Standard Timing Report

Let's look at a realistic mock timing report snippet for a setup time check. We will dissect the columns and the data presented.

**************************************** Report : timing -path full -delay max -max_paths 1 Design : my_module **************************************** Startpoint: reg_A (rising edge-triggered flip-flop clocked by clk) Endpoint: reg_B (rising edge-triggered flip-flop clocked by clk) Path Group: clk Path Type: max Point Incr Path -------------------------------------------------------------------------- clock clk (rise edge) 0.00 0.00 clock network delay (ideal) 0.00 0.00 reg_A/CP (DFFQX1) 0.00 0.00 r reg_A/Q (DFFQX1) 0.15 0.15 f U1/Y (AND2X2) 0.20 0.35 f U2/Y (OR2X1) 0.25 0.60 f U3/Y (XOR2X1) 0.45 1.05 r reg_B/D (DFFQX1) 0.05 1.10 r data arrival time 1.10 clock clk (rise edge) 2.00 2.00 clock network delay (ideal) 0.00 2.00 clock reconvergence pessimism 0.00 2.00 clock uncertainty -0.10 1.90 reg_B/CP (DFFQX1) 0.00 1.90 r library setup time -0.08 1.82 data required time 1.82 -------------------------------------------------------------------------- data required time 1.82 data arrival time -1.10 -------------------------------------------------------------------------- slack (MET) 0.72 --------------------------------------------------------------------------

This report is divided into a few key sections:

  • Header: Specifies the report conditions (e.g., max delay for setup, min delay for hold) and the design name.
  • Startpoint and Endpoint: The launch and capture flip-flops (or ports) of the path. It also shows the clock domains for each.
  • Data Arrival Path (Launch): Traces the signal from the launch clock edge at the Startpoint to the D pin of the Endpoint.
  • Data Required Path (Capture): Calculates when the data must arrive at the Endpoint to meet the setup (or hold) requirement, based on the capture clock edge.
  • Slack Calculation: Subtracts Arrival Time from Required Time (for setup). Positive slack means the path meets timing!
Decoding the Columns

The core of the report is the tabular data. Let's break down the typical columns:

1. Point (Node)

This column lists every pin, port, or logical checkpoint along the path. It typically shows the instance name followed by the pin name (e.g., U1/Y is the Y output pin of instance U1). The parenthesis usually contains the standard cell name from the technology library (e.g., AND2X2).

2. Incr (Incremental Delay)

The Incremental Delay is the delay introduced by that specific component or net. If the point is an output pin (like U1/Y), the Incr value is the gate delay from the input pin to the output pin, plus the net delay to the next point. This is the most critical column for finding bottlenecks. A huge spike in this column means a specific gate is extremely slow or driving a massive load (high capacitance).

3. Path (Cumulative Delay)

The Path column is a running total of the Incr values. It shows the absolute time elapsed since the reference clock edge. The r or f appended to the Path value indicates whether the signal is making a rise (r) or fall (f) transition at that node. Transitions matter because cell delays depend heavily on whether they are pulling the output high or low.

Identifying the Bottleneck

When a path violates timing (resulting in a negative slack, or VIOLATED), your job is to find out why. Here is a systematic approach to identifying bottlenecks using the report:

  1. Scan the Incr Column: Look for unusually large numbers. If most gates have delays of 0.05ns - 0.1ns, but one gate has an Incr of 0.8ns, you've found your primary suspect.
  2. Check the Fan-out/Load: An unusually high Incr delay often implies high capacitive load. A tiny gate driving 50 other gates will struggle to change its output voltage quickly. (Note: Many reports optionally include a 'Cap' or 'Fanout' column to verify this).
  3. Look at Logic Depth: Count the number of cells in the data path. If there are 30 levels of logic between two flip-flops running at 1GHz, no amount of gate sizing will save you. The logic must be pipelined (split with a flip-flop) or structurally optimized.
  4. Check Transition Times (Slew): Slow transitions propagate through a path, making subsequent gates even slower. Look for points where a signal changes from 'r' to 'f' but the delay is massive.
  5. Analyze the Clock Path: Sometimes the data path is fine, but the clock path is skewed. Look at the "clock network delay" on both the launch and capture sides. If the capture clock is arriving extremely early compared to the launch clock (negative skew for setup), it will artificially squeeze your timing window.

In our mock report above, look at U3/Y (XOR2X1). The incremental delay is 0.45, which is significantly larger than the delay of other gates. If this path were failing, U3 would be the prime target for optimization—perhaps by swapping it for a higher-drive cell (like XOR2X4) or reducing its load.
