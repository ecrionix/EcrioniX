Fixing Hold Violations

Understand why hold violations occur, why we defer fixing them until after CTS, and the delicate balance of buffer insertion and cell downsizing without breaking setup timing.

The "Too Fast" Problem

As we covered in previous modules, a hold violation occurs when a data path is too fast. The data arrives at the capturing flip-flop before the previous data has been safely latched during the required hold window.

Unlike setup violations, which are heavily influenced by the clock period (frequency), hold violations are strictly a race between the data path delay and the clock skew. If the launch clock reaches its flip-flop and propogates the data faster than the capture clock reaches its flip-flop, the data can overwrite the old value prematurely.

The Golden Rule: Wait Until After CTS

One of the most common mistakes junior physical design engineers make is attempting to fix hold violations during the early placement phases. Never fix hold violations before Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS).

Why Wait?
Before CTS, the clock network is ideal. The timing engine assumes clock skew is zero (or uses an estimated blanket value). Because hold violations are hyper-sensitive to clock skew, the pre-CTS hold reports are essentially fiction.

If you insert buffers to fix hold violations pre-CTS, you are optimizing against fake data. Once the actual clock tree is synthesized and real buffers are placed, the actual clock skew will emerge. This real skew might naturally fix the hold violation, or it might make it worse. If you fixed it pre-CTS, you likely added unnecessary buffers, bloating the design's area and power consumption for no reason.

Workflow standard: Focus purely on setup violations before CTS. After CTS (during routing and post-route optimization), switch gears and fix the real hold violations.

Strategy 1: Inserting Delay Buffers

The most direct way to fix a path that is too fast is to physically slow it down. We achieve this by inserting delay buffers (or sometimes pairs of inverters) directly into the combinational data path.

By padding the data path with additional gate delay, we push the arrival time of the data transition out of the danger zone (the hold window).

When the EDA tool (like Innovus, ICC2, or Tempus) performs hold fixing, it searches the physical layout for a valid location to drop a buffer along the failing net. The tool will calculate the exact amount of delay needed and choose a buffer cell with the appropriate drive strength to satisfy the `T_hold` requirement.

Strategy 2: Downsizing Cells

What if the design is densely packed, and there is simply no physical area to insert a new buffer without causing routing congestion or overlaps? Alternatively, we might want to fix the hold violation without adding the leakage power of an entirely new cell.

In this case, we use Cell Downsizing.

Combinational logic gates in standard cell libraries come in various drive strengths (e.g., X1, X2, X4, X8). A cell with a higher drive strength switches faster (lower delay) but consumes more power and area.

To slow down the data path, we can swap a fast, high-drive cell for a slower, low-drive cell. For example, swapping a NOR2_X4 for a NOR2_X1 increases the gate delay and the transition time, pushing the data arrival safely past the hold window—all without adding a new instance to the netlist.

The Ultimate Risk: Breaking Setup Timing

Timing optimization is a delicate balancing act. The fundamental equation of a timing path dictates that the total data path delay must fit inside a specific window:

T_hold_req < T_data_path < T_setup_req

When we add delay to fix a hold violation (either by inserting buffers or downsizing cells), we are increasing T_data_path.

Ping-Pong Violations:
If a path has very little positive setup slack (margin), adding a buffer to fix a hold violation might push the total delay past T_setup_req, instantly creating a setup violation.

Consider the following post-CTS timing report snippet:

Path 1: Launch FF -> Capture FF (Hold Check) ----------------------------------------------------------- Hold Required Time : 0.15 ns Data Arrival Time : 0.10 ns Slack (VIOLATED) : -0.05 ns Path 1: Launch FF -> Capture FF (Setup Check) ----------------------------------------------------------- Setup Required Time : 1.20 ns Data Arrival Time : 1.18 ns (Using the un-buffered fast path) Slack (MET) : 0.02 ns

In the above scenario, we have a hold violation of -0.05 ns. We need to add at least 0.05 ns of delay to fix it.

However, our setup slack is only +0.02 ns. If we add a 0.06 ns buffer to fix the hold violation, the new Data Arrival Time becomes 1.24 ns (1.18 + 0.06). This would cause a setup violation of -0.04 ns (1.20 - 1.24).

This path is over-constrained. The Setup-Hold Window is too narrow. When tools encounter this, they might leave the hold violation untouched to avoid breaking setup, or they might attempt more complex fixes like:

  • Useful Skew: Deliberately shifting the clock arrival time at the capture flip-flop to widen the window.
  • Logic Restructuring: Re-synthesizing the logic to create completely different paths.
  • VT Swapping: Changing threshold voltage variants (e.g., LVT to HVT) for finer delay granularity.
