8b/10b Encoding

The brilliant IBM engineering hack from 1983 that became the backbone of PCIe, USB, SATA, and Gigabit Ethernet.

The Problem: The Need for Embedded Clocking

In low-speed electronic protocols like I2C, SPI, or a simple UART, you usually rely on separate wires for Data and Clock (or a perfectly matched baud rate). The Clock wire literally ticks up and down, telling the receiver exactly when to sample the Data wire.

But when you want to transmit data at Gigabits per second (like a PCIe bus or a USB cable), a separate Clock wire becomes a nightmare. High-frequency clock wires act like massive antennas, radiating electromagnetic interference (EMI) everywhere. Furthermore, the Clock wire and Data wire might be physically slightly different lengths, meaning the signals arrive out of sync (Clock Skew).

The industry's solution was Embedded Clocking. High-speed serial links drop the Clock wire entirely. They transmit only Data over a single differential pair (like TX+ and TX-). The receiver uses a specialized analog circuit called a Clock Data Recovery (CDR) Loop.

Clock Data Recovery (CDR)
A phase-locked loop (PLL) inside the receiver that watches the data line for transitions (0→1 or 1→0). It essentially "steals" the timing information from the data edges and synchronizes its own internal clock to those exact edges.

The "Long Zero" Death

CDR sounds perfect, but it has a fatal flaw. What happens if you need to transmit a black pixel, a silent audio file, or a zeroed-out memory sector? Your raw data might literally be thousands of consecutive 0s.

If the data line just stays flat at 0 (or 1) for too long, there are no electrical transitions. Without edges, the receiver's CDR circuit goes blind. Its internal clock drifts, loses synchronization, and starts sampling garbage data.

Electrical Danger: DC Drift

If you send too many 1s or 0s in a row, it's not just a clocking problem. The physical wire acts like a capacitor. A long string of 1s will literally charge up the wire, shifting the average DC voltage so high that the receiver can no longer distinguish between a 1 and a 0. The wire must remain DC Balanced (spending 50% of its time HIGH and 50% of its time LOW).

The Solution: 8b/10b Encoding

Invented by IBM engineer Albert Widmer, 8b/10b encoding elegantly solves both the Clock Recovery problem and the DC Balance problem in one hardware block.

Instead of transmitting the raw 8-bit bytes, a hardware encoder intercepts every 8-bit byte and maps it to a specific 10-bit symbol before pushing it out onto the wire.

Expanding 8 bits (256 possible values) into 10 bits (1024 possible values) gives us a massive pool of extra symbols. Out of those 1024 possible 10-bit patterns, we throw away all the "bad" ones and only keep the patterns that follow strict rules:

  • Maximum Run Length of 5: We discard any 10-bit symbol that has more than five consecutive 0s or 1s. This guarantees the receiver will always see an edge to synchronize its clock, even if the raw data is completely blank.
  • Perfect Balance: We discard symbols that have a massive imbalance of 1s and 0s. We only keep symbols that have exactly five 0s and five 1s, OR symbols that have six of one and four of the other.

Notice how the flatline raw data is magically translated into a signal with plenty of edges for the receiver to lock onto!

DC Balance (Running Disparity)

To keep the electrical wire perfectly balanced at 50% high and 50% low, the hardware encoder tracks a state machine called Running Disparity (RD). It remembers if it has transmitted more 1s or more 0s recently.

Because some 10-bit symbols have six 1s and four 0s (they are "1-heavy"), every 8-bit byte is actually mapped to two valid 10-bit codes in the lookup table:

  1. A version with positive disparity (e.g., six 1s, four 0s).
  2. A version with negative disparity (e.g., four 1s, six 0s).

If the wire is currently leaning positive, the hardware automatically selects the "0-heavy" version for the next byte to pull the wire back down to a perfect neutral balance.

Control Codes (The K-Characters)

Since 8 bits only require 256 mapped values, and we have plenty of valid 10-bit patterns left over in our pool, we use the remaining patterns as Hardware Control Commands.

These special patterns are called K-Characters (Control Characters). Because these 10-bit patterns can never accidentally occur during normal data transmission, the receiving hardware instantly recognizes them as structural commands.

For example, PCIe and Gigabit Ethernet use specific K-Characters for:

  • K28.5 (Comma): Sent repeatedly when the link is idle to help the receiver align its byte boundaries.
  • Start of Packet (SOP): Alerts the receiver that a data payload is beginning.
  • End of Packet (EOP): Alerts the receiver that the payload has finished.

The 3b/4b + 5b/6b Split

Under the hood, a single massive 256-to-1024 lookup table is too slow and requires too many gates. IBM engineers cleverly split the 8-bit byte into a 5-bit block and a 3-bit block. They map the 5 bits into 6 bits (5b/6b encoding) and the 3 bits into 4 bits (3b/4b encoding), then concatenate them. This allows the encoder to be implemented in ultra-fast combinational logic.
